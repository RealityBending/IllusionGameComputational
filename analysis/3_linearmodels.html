IllusionComputationalModel - Linear Models

Study 2 replaces the smooth surfaces of DDM-5, the model retained in Study 1 (2_modelcomparison.qmd), with linear effects of task difficulty and illusion strength, so that every effect, and every participant’s sensitivity to the illusion, is one interpretable coefficient. This script has two parts:

  • Model Specification: how the linear predictors were chosen, from the Study 1 fits. It reads only their prediction files (./hpc predict, then ./hpc pull predictions), never a fit, like 2_modelcomparison.qmd.
  • Convergence, and what follows, once the linear models are fitted.

The Study 1 Surfaces

The linear models are specified from DDM-5’s parameter surfaces in Study 1: for each parameter, its population-level value over a 40 x 40 grid of task difficulty x illusion strength (the heatmaps of 2_modelcomparison.qmd). A linear model acts on each parameter’s linear predictor, so the surfaces are taken back to that scale, through the link function of the parameter.

The Three Ranges of the Predictors

Task difficulty and illusion strength are used over three nested ranges (server/AGENT.md §6.17):

  • The full range: every value shown in any study. The Study 1 models are fitted to every trial, over this range.
  • The reliable predictor space (the reliable subrange): the full range less the values that only one study showed. Every prediction and figure, of Study 1 and Study 2, is made over it (plot_ranges in server/predictions.R).
  • The linear subrange: the part of the reliable predictor space that the linear models of Study 2 are fitted on, where the effects of the two predictors are taken to be close enough to linear (igc_linear_ranges in server/models.R). It is still to be refined, and for now it is the whole reliable predictor space.

The Full Range, Study by Study

The studies did not all show the same stimuli. They used three designs (table below). Validation, the first, showed the strongest illusions, and on Müller-Lyer and Ebbinghaus also the largest differences. EEG and Reliability used a narrower grid. The four later studies (International, Suggestibility, Phenomenological Control and Self-Association) used the main one, which contains the EEG and Reliability grid. Every study showed 8 levels of difference and 8 or 10 of strength. The levels that only Validation showed were seen by about 250 participants, in about 4,000 trials per level, against 10,000 to 27,000 trials per level elsewhere. What is estimated there rests on one study, which the GAMs of Study 1, with no effect of study, cannot tell apart from what is particular to it.

The Reliable Predictor Space

The figures and the linear fits are therefore limited to a reliable predictor space, by one rule for the three illusions: at each end of each predictor, the most extreme values that together hold less than 5% of the illusion’s trials are left out. The difference is kept from its 5th to its 95th percentile, and the absolute strength up to its 95th percentile. On all three illusions this is exactly the range of the main design. What it leaves out is the levels that only Validation showed, 38% to 53% of that study’s trials, and none of the other studies’ trials. Every curve and heatmap of Study 1 and Study 2 is drawn over this space (plot_ranges in server/predictions.R, which the code below checks against the data).

# The reliable predictor space at threshold p: the difference from its p-th to
# its (1 - p)-th quantile and the absolute strength up to its (1 - p)-th, over
# every trial of the illusion (the difference is absolute already)
reliable_space <- function(p = 0.05) {
  df |>
    summarise(Difference_from = quantile(Illusion_Difference, p, type = 1),
              Difference_to = quantile(Illusion_Difference, 1 - p, type = 1),
              Strength = quantile(abs(Illusion_Strength), 1 - p, type = 1),
              .by = Illusion_Type) |>
    arrange(Illusion_Type)
}

The Linear Subrange

The linear models are fitted over a linear subrange of each illusion, within the reliable predictor space. This is the set of stimuli over which the effects of the two predictors are taken to be close enough to linear for a linear model to stand in for DDM-5’s surfaces. It is set in igc_linear_ranges in server/models.R, and the registry’s linear models take their range from it.

The linear subrange is still to be refined. For now it is the whole reliable predictor space, on all three illusions. With the coding chosen below, the least linear parameter keeps an R² of .87 to .89 over it. A narrower subrange would trade trials for linearity, and the criterion for that is not decided. The boxes drawn in Study 1 for the signed coding of strength (Müller-Lyer 0.1–0.4 x ±30, drawn by eye) are candidates (server/AGENT.md §6.17). A narrower subrange shows in the figures: the linear model’s lines stop short of the edges of the reliable predictor space, and 2_modelcomparison.qmd outlines the subrange on its heatmaps.

For Müller-Lyer, the reliable predictor space starts at a difference of 0.045, the hardest level of the main design (8% of its trials), where the range of Study 1 started at 0.05. That level is the least linear part of its surfaces: without it, the worst parameter of the coding retained below is at .917 rather than .887 (the non-decision time), as the surfaces bend most at the hardest discriminations. It is one candidate for a narrower linear subrange.

How Linear a Coding Is

A coding of the two predictors is judged by how much of each parameter’s surface a linear model in its terms reproduces: the R² of the surface, on its link scale, regressed on those terms. It is computed in two ways:

  • At the stimuli, weighted by their number of trials. This is what the linear model is fitted to, and the one used for the choices below. The stimuli are not spread evenly over the space, so the two differ.
  • Over the grid, every point weighted the same, as when the Study 1 boxes were drawn.

The value at a stimulus is interpolated (bilinearly) between the four grid points around it. Each coding is summarised by its worst parameter, since the model is only as linear as its least linear parameter, and by the mean over the five.

Code 
# A surface's value at any (S, D), by bilinear interpolation between the four
# grid points around it. `h` is one parameter of one illusion.
surface_at <- function(h, S, D) {
  gx <- sort(unique(h$Illusion_Strength))
  gy <- sort(unique(h$Illusion_Difference))
  z <- matrix(NA_real_, length(gx), length(gy))
  z[cbind(match(h$Illusion_Strength, gx), match(h$Illusion_Difference, gy))] <- h$Link
  i <- pmin(pmax(findInterval(S, gx), 1), length(gx) - 1)
  j <- pmin(pmax(findInterval(D, gy), 1), length(gy) - 1)
  tx <- (S - gx[i]) / (gx[i + 1] - gx[i])
  ty <- (D - gy[j]) / (gy[j + 1] - gy[j])
  z[cbind(i, j)] * (1 - tx) * (1 - ty) + z[cbind(i + 1, j)] * tx * (1 - ty) +
    z[cbind(i, j + 1)] * (1 - tx) * ty + z[cbind(i + 1, j + 1)] * tx * ty
}

# R² of one coding on one illusion, per parameter. A coding is a function of
# the raw difference D and signed strength S that returns the model's terms
# (without the intercept).
linearity <- function(illusion, coding) {
  st <- filter(stimuli, Illusion_Type == illusion)
  map_dfr(names(ddm_links), \(p) {
    h <- filter(surfaces, Illusion_Type == illusion, Parameter == p)
    g <- filter(h, in_range(illusion, Illusion_Difference, Illusion_Strength))
    y <- surface_at(h, st$Strength, st$Difference)
    data.frame(
      Illusion_Type = illusion, Parameter = p,
      R2_trials = summary(lm(y ~ coding(st$Difference, st$Strength), weights = st$n))$r.squared,
      R2_grid = summary(lm(g$Link ~ coding(g$Illusion_Difference, g$Illusion_Strength)))$r.squared
    )
  })
}

# Worst and mean over the parameters
summarise_linearity <- function(r) {
  summarise(r, Worst = min(R2_trials), Worst_Parameter = ddm_labels[Parameter[which.min(R2_trials)]],
            Mean = mean(R2_trials), Worst_Grid = min(R2_grid), Mean_Grid = mean(R2_grid),
            .by = any_of(c("Illusion_Type", "Coding", "Transform_D", "Power_S")))
}

Coding of Illusion Strength

Study 1 coded strength as one signed variable, positive when the illusion works against the correct answer (conflicting) and negative when it works for it (facilitating). Three codings are compared:

  • Difference × Strength: signed strength, one slope across both sides.
  • Difference × Strength × Effect, no gap: strength in absolute value, and whether it is conflicting or facilitating, coded +0.5 / −0.5, which enters only through its interactions with strength (Strength:Effect and Difference:Strength:Effect). Each side has its own slope, and the two lines meet at zero strength. The value of Effect on the trials with no illusion does not matter, as it is multiplied by a strength of 0 there.
  • Difference × Strength × Effect, with a gap: the full product, with Effect and Difference:Effect as well (Effect 0 for the trials with no illusion), so that the two lines need not meet at zero strength.

Transformations of Difficulty and Strength

The two predictors are physical quantities, whose perceived magnitude need not be proportional to them, and need not scale in the same way from one illusion to another. The difference is a Weber fraction in all three illusions: the size of the larger target relative to the smaller, minus 1 (Pyllusion, (1 + difference) * size_min), of the lengths of the lines in Müller-Lyer and Vertical-Horizontal and of the diameters of the inner circles in Ebbinghaus. The strength is an angle in Müller-Lyer (of the fins) and Vertical-Horizontal (of the rotation of one line towards the vertical), and the size of the surrounding circles relative to the target in Ebbinghaus.

Two kinds of transformation of the difference were searched. Powers, from the log (the most compressive) to the square, are the empirical family. The log of the ratio of the two targets, log(1 + d), is the one Weber’s law predicts: if each target is encoded with noise proportional to its size, the evidence that one is larger grows with the log of their ratio, and the drift rate with it. Over these ranges it is only mildly compressive (at d = 0.7, log(1.7) = 0.53). The absolute strength was raised to powers from 0.25 to 2.

ISI therefore enters the model centred on its study’s window, (ISI - centre) / 0.25, from −1 at the start of the window to 1 at its end in every study. Its effect is then estimated only from the variation within each study, which was randomised from trial to trial, and is not confounded with the study, nor extrapolated beyond the range a study covers (above 1.1 s, the absolute ISI comes from the EEG study alone). The absolute level of each window is taken up by the study’s intercept (below), with everything else that differs between studies.

It enters every parameter, the outlier proportion included, as a quadratic, poly(ISI, 2, raw = TRUE), for the levelling off: the two terms are then ISI and its square in these units, whose slopes the priors of the other predictors suit, and which need no basis carried over to predict new values. An orthogonal poly(ISI, 2) would not do: its columns have a length of 1 whatever the unit of ISI, so a standard deviation of about 0.002 on 200,000 trials, against which the slope priors would hold the effect of ISI near zero, and differently for every number of trials.

The Studies

The seven studies differ in their settings, samples and procedures (overall error rates from 18% to 24%), and every participant took part in one of them. Each parameter, the outlier proportion included, gets a random intercept per study, (1 | Study). The participants’ own effects are then deviations from their study’s mean rather than from the grand mean, which matters for the correlations between illusions: without it, the differences between studies would be shared by a participant’s effects on all three illusions, and inflate their correlations. With seven studies, it is kept to intercepts.

The same formula for the five parameters of DDM-5 (lin_ddm5 in server/models.R), with every effect varying by participant and an intercept varying by study:

# The same terms on every parameter:
#   Difference * Strength + Strength:Effect + Difference:Strength:Effect
brms::bf(
  RT | dec(Error) ~ Difference * Strength + Strength:Effect + Difference:Strength:Effect +
                    poly(ISI, 2, raw = TRUE) +
                    (1 + Difference * Strength + Strength:Effect + Difference:Strength:Effect | Participant) +
                    (1 | Study),
  boundary        ~ ...,  # the same
  bias            ~ ...,
  ndt             ~ ...,
  sigmadrift      ~ ...,
  sigmabias = 0,
  sigmandt = 0,
  poutlier        ~ poly(ISI, 2, raw = TRUE) + (1 | Participant) + (1 | Study),
  family = cogmod::cogmod_ddm()
)

With absolute strength and its side coded ±0.5, entering only through its interactions with strength, the coefficients read: the intercept is the parameter at zero strength, where the two sides meet; Strength:Effect is the difference between the conflicting and the facilitating slope, i.e. the sensitivity to the illusion; Strength is their mean, i.e. how much more (or less) a conflicting illusion hurts than a facilitating one helps; and each Difference: term is how one of these changes with task difficulty. Strength:Effect is also half the signed strength, so the model is Study 1’s signed coding with a V-shaped term in absolute strength added.

plot_approximation <- function(curves) {
  # The rows, in two groups: what can be observed, and what the model explains
  # it with
  groups <- list(
    "Observable Outcomes" = c("Error Rate" = "Error rate", "Median RT" = "Median RT\n(correct, s)"),
    "Model Parameters" = c("Drift" = "Drift rate\n(+ towards correct)", "Drift Variability" = "Drift rate\nvariability",
                           "Boundary" = "Boundary\nseparation", "Bias" = "Starting point\n(0 error, 1 correct)",
                           "NDT" = "Non-decision\ntime (s)")
  )
  panels <- unlist(unname(groups))

  # The difficulty levels differ between illusions: each curve is coloured by
  # its difference, on a scale from the hardest of the GAM's levels (0) to the
  # easiest (1), which are evenly spaced. A linear curve at the same level as a
  # GAM curve takes its colour, and one predicted on another grid (made before
  # plot_ranges last changed) the colour of its own difficulty.
  range_of <- curves |>
    filter(Type == "GAM") |>
    summarise(Lo = min(Illusion_Difference), Hi = max(Illusion_Difference), .by = Illusion_Type)
  d <- curves |>
    left_join(range_of, by = "Illusion_Type") |>
    mutate(Difficulty = pmin(pmax((Illusion_Difference - Lo) / (Hi - Lo), 0), 1),
           Predicted = case_when(Parameter == "mu" ~ -Predicted,
                                 Parameter == "bias" ~ 1 - Predicted,
                                 .default = Predicted),

           Illusion = factor(illusions[Illusion_Type], levels = illusions),
           Type = factor(Type, levels = c("GAM", "Linear")),

           # linear model
           Line = paste(Type, round(Illusion_Difference, 5), sign(coalesce(Effect, 0))))

  # plot_retained() has them dashed, which here are the linear models.
  refs <- data.frame(Parameter = panels[c("Drift", "Bias")], y = c(0, 0.5))

  # Each illusion's x-axis over its reliable predictor space, whatever is
  # drawn on it: an invisible point at each end, in every row, at a value the
  # row already has

  # so that there is a gap between them. Their columns line up, so the
  # illusions are named once, above the first, and the axis of illusion

    rows <- groups[[name]]
    first <- name == names(groups)[1]
    last <- name == names(groups)[length(groups)]

    ggplot(in_block(d), aes(Illusion_Strength, Predicted)) +
      geom_blank(data = in_block(ends)) +
      geom_vline(xintercept = 0, colour = "grey70", linewidth = 0.3) +
      geom_hline(data = in_block(refs), aes(yintercept = y), colour = "grey60", linewidth = 0.4,

      geom_line(aes(colour = Difficulty, group = Line, linetype = Type, linewidth = Type), na.rm = TRUE) +
      scale_colour_gradientn(colours = c("#D84315", "#FF9800", "#FFC107", "#8BC34A", "#2E7D32"),
                             limits = c(0, 1), breaks = c(0, 1), labels = c("hardest", "easiest"),
                             guide = guide_colourbar(title.position = "left", barwidth = grid::unit(6, "lines"),
                                                     barheight = grid::unit(0.5, "lines"), order = 1)) +
      scale_linetype_manual(values = c(GAM = "solid", Linear = "22"), drop = FALSE,
                            labels = c(GAM = "GAM (Study 1)", Linear = "Linear (Study 2)"),
                            guide = guide_legend(title = NULL, order = 2,
                                                 override.aes = list(colour = "grey25"))) +
      scale_linewidth_manual(values = c(GAM = 0.55, Linear = 0.5), guide = "none") +
      facet_grid(Parameter ~ Illusion, scales = "free", switch = "y") +
      labs(y = NULL, colour = "Task difficulty ",
           x = if (last) "Illusion strength (negative: congruent, positive: incongruent)") +

            legend.key.width = grid::unit(1.6, "lines"),
            legend.margin = margin(t = -4),

  }

The Effect of the Interval Before the Stimulus

What the linear models make of the ISI (see “The Interval Before the Stimulus” above). Each parameter has the same quadratic in the position of the ISI within its study’s window, plus a random intercept per study. The curves below are each study’s, with its own intercept and the participant at the population level, along its own window in seconds: the window’s centre plus 0.25 s times the coded ISI, as fit_model.R codes it. They are drawn at three stimuli: no illusion, at the hardest, the middle and the easiest difficulty of the range fitted. On the link scale the ISI moves every parameter by the same amount at every stimulus, so a parameter’s curves differ between the three columns only in their level; the error rate and the median RT depend on the stimulus too. As in the figure of the linear approximation, the drift is positive towards the correct response and the starting point runs from 0 at the error boundary to 1 at the correct one.

The predictions are made on the cluster (compute_isi_curves() in server/predictions.R) and stored in the linear models’ prediction files, since 2026-11-25. For now only the 50-participant test of the Müller-Lyer model has them (server/AGENT.md §6.15), whose studies are those its 50 participants come from: the study intercepts of the studies with few of them rest on little.
