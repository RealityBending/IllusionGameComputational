IllusionComputationalModel - Reliability

The linear models of Study 2 (3_linearmodels.qmd) give every participant their own effects of task difficulty and of illusion strength, and of the two together, on each parameter of the DDM. This script asks how reliable these participant-level effects are: how well they tell the participants apart, given how uncertain each participant’s estimate is. Those that do are the candidates for measuring individual differences in sensitivity to illusions.

Like the other scripts, it never loads a fit. The participants’ effects are summarised on the cluster, from the draws of the best linear model of each illusion (compute_grouplevel() in server/predictions.R, run by ./hpc predict), and stored in the model’s prediction file, which is pulled with ./hpc pull predictions. The full fits hold some 70,000 participant-level parameters each, and were made with brms 2.21.0 (server/AGENT.md §3.9).

Warning

For now this runs on the 50-participant tests of the Müller-Lyer and Vertical-Horizontal models (untransformed, server/AGENT.md §6.15): the pipeline, not the results. Fifty participants estimate neither the variability between participants nor the reliability well, and the Ebbinghaus test is not predicted yet.

The Participants’ Effects

Each parameter of the linear models has, per participant, a deviation from the population for each of its terms (the random effects): an intercept, the difference, the absolute strength, the absolute strength by its direction (Effect, +0.5 when the illusion is incongruent, −0.5 when it is congruent), and the interactions of the difference with the last two. The raw terms are not the most interpretable, since the effect of the strength is split between Strength and Strength:Effect. The effects studied here are the baseline of each parameter (its intercept) and combinations of the other terms, made draw by draw on the cluster:

The predictors are coded as in the fits: the difference from −1 to 1 and the absolute strength from 0 to 1 over the range fitted, so the slope of the strength is the change from no illusion to the strongest illusion of the range. Each effect is a deviation of the participant from the population, on the link scale of its parameter, and the baseline a deviation from the participant’s study, which has an intercept of its own. The outlier proportion has a baseline only. Baselines can be expected to be the most reliable: every trial informs them, where a slope rests on the contrast between trials.

The model-based PSE is the one effect that is not a deviation. It is computed draw by draw from the participant’s total coefficients on the drift (population + study + participant), in the raw units of the difference, at the same strength as the empirical PSE below (igc_pse_strength, half the strongest of the range). It is the illusion’s strength expressed as the physical difference it cancels. A precision common to all of a participant’s drift coefficients cancels out of it. The raw strength slope keeps that precision: participants who see differences more sharply have steeper slopes for real and illusory differences alike. The PSE thus measures susceptibility relative to the participant’s own discrimination.

Reliability: D-vour

D-vour, the variability-over-uncertainty ratio (performance::performance_dvour()), compares how much the participants’ estimates differ from one another with how uncertain each of them is:

\[ \text{D-vour} = \frac{\sigma_B^2}{\sigma_B^2 + \mu_{\text{SE}}^2} \]

where \(\sigma_B\) is the standard deviation, across participants, of their point estimates (posterior medians), and \(\mu_{\text{SE}}\) the mean of their posterior dispersions (MADs). It is 0 when the differences between participants are no larger than the uncertainty of each, and tends to 1 when the uncertainty is negligible next to them. Above 0.75 the differences between participants are at least three times the uncertainty; around 0.5 the two are similar, and the estimates should be used with caution; below 0.5 the uncertainty dominates (the performance documentation). It rests on the estimates alone, so it is itself more reliable with more participants.

Code 
# D-vour of every effect of every parameter
dvour_of <- function(tables) {
  imap_dfr(tables, \(g, ill) mutate(performance::performance_dvour(g), Illusion_Type = ill)) |>
    mutate(Illusion = factor(illusions[Illusion_Type], levels = illusions),
           Model_Parameter = factor(parameter_labels[Component], levels = parameter_labels),
           Effect = Parameter)
}
dvour <- dvour_of(map(grouplevel, "effects")) |>
  mutate(Effect = factor(Effect, levels = igc_grouplevel_effect_names))

  summarise(Lowest = min(D_vour), .by = c(Component, Parameter)) |>
  filter(Lowest > reliable_threshold)

dvour_wide <- function(d) {

Empirical Indices

The usual way to measure individual differences in this task is from the behaviour directly: how often and how fast a participant responds, overall and when the illusion works against them, and where their psychometric function lies. These indices are computed here for every participant, on each illusion, from the trials the linear models are fitted on (the linear subrange, igc_linear_ranges), so that the next section can relate them to the participants’ model parameters, with the same trials behind both.

The psychometric function is a logistic regression of each participant’s errors on the difference between the targets and the signed strength of the illusion (positive when incongruent), on one illusion. Where the error rate is 50%, the participant cannot tell the targets apart. With no illusion, the slope of the difference gives the JND. Under an incongruent illusion, the difference at which the errors reach 50% is the point of subjective equality: the physical difference the illusion cancels, in the units of the difference. It is taken at half the strongest incongruent strength of the range: at the strongest, it lies beyond the largest difference shown for about one participant in six on the Müller-Lyer and Vertical-Horizontal illusions, where it is extrapolated. The function is linear in both predictors, as a first approximation. It is left missing when the participant made fewer than five errors or the fit fails, and when it is degenerate. That covers errors that do not decrease as the difference grows, and a JND beyond the largest difference shown (the errors barely depend on the difference). It also covers a JND below a tenth of the smallest (the errors switch from all to none between two levels, so the slope is not estimable).

Code 
# Every participant's trials on each illusion, within its linear subrange
# (the rule of igc_linear_data(), which fit_model.R applies)
ranges <- imap_dfr(igc_linear_ranges, \(r, ill) {
  tibble(Illusion_Type = ill, D_lo = r$difference[1], D_hi = r$difference[2], S_max = r$strength)
})
trials <- map_dfr(1:3, \(i) read.csv(paste0(data_path, "illusion_part", i, ".csv"))) |>
  mutate(Illusion_Difference = abs(Illusion_Difference)) |>
  inner_join(ranges, by = "Illusion_Type") |>
  filter(Illusion_Difference >= D_lo - 1e-9, Illusion_Difference <= D_hi + 1e-9,
         abs(Illusion_Strength) <= S_max + 1e-9)

# A participant's psychometric function on one illusion (see above): the PSE
# of an incongruent illusion of half the strongest strength of the range, and
# the JND, or NA when the function is degenerate
psychometric <- function(d, s_max, d_lo, d_hi) {
  na <- c(PSE = NA_real_, JND = NA_real_)
  if (sum(d$Error) < 5) return(na)
  fit <- tryCatch(suppressWarnings(glm(Error ~ Illusion_Difference + Illusion_Strength,
                                       family = binomial, data = d)),
                  error = \(e) NULL)
  if (is.null(fit) || !fit$converged) return(na)
  b <- coef(fit)
  if (anyNA(b) || b[["Illusion_Difference"]] >= 0) return(na)
  jnd <- log(3) / -b[["Illusion_Difference"]]
  if (jnd > d_hi || jnd < d_lo / 10) return(na)
  # At the strength of the model-based PSE (igc_pse_strength, server/predictions.R)
  c(PSE = -(b[["(Intercept)"]] + b[["Illusion_Strength"]] * s_max * igc_pse_strength) /
      b[["Illusion_Difference"]],
    JND = jnd)
}

Structure Analysis

How the model’s participant-level effects relate to one another and to the empirical indices, on each illusion: one correlation matrix per illusion, of the model effects reliable on every illusion (a D-vour above reliable_threshold on each, above) and the empirical indices, over the participants of that illusion’s model. The matrices hold the same variables, in the same order, so that they can be compared cell by cell. The question is whether the structure is the same on every illusion. The order is a hierarchical clustering of the absolute correlations averaged over the illusions (correlation::cor_sort() on 1 − mean |r|), so that variables that go together sit together, whether they rise or fall together.

Three choices: - The correlations are Spearman’s, since several indices are skewed (RTs, IES, the PSE and JND). - Every variable is centred within the participant’s study first, on each illusion. The model’s baselines are deviations from the study’s intercept, while the empirical indices also carry the differences between studies. Centring both leaves the differences between participants of the same study only, on both sides. - The illusions are not in the same matrix. Correlating them, participant by participant, is for the dimension scores, once they are extracted from these matrices. It needs the participants of one illusion’s model to be in the others’ too, which the full fits will have (the tests drew their 50 participants on each illusion independently).

The empirical indices have no reliability of their own here, so a weak correlation with one of them may only mean that it is noisy.

Dimensions

The matrices above show blocks of variables that go together. This section makes them dimensions and asks whether they are the same on every illusion, in two steps: - Exploratory Graph Analysis (EGA) finds the dimensions of each illusion, and how stable they are. - A confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) of the structure common to the illusions tests whether it holds on each illusion and across illusions, and gives each participant a score on each dimension, illusion by illusion.

On the 50-participant tests this sets up the pipeline only. EGA and its bootstraps are unstable at that size, and the cross-illusion analyses need the same participants in every model, which only the full fits will have.

Redundant Variables

Several variables are near-duplicates: the drift’s baseline and difficulty, or the median RT of incongruent trials and the illusion effect on RT, which is computed from it. A pair that shares most of its connections in the network forms a dimension of its own, and inflates the one it belongs to. Unique Variable Analysis (EGAnet::UVA()) flags the pairs whose weighted topological overlap (wTO) exceeds uva_cutoff. Of each redundant set, it keeps the variable least redundant with the rest (the lowest maximum wTO with the other variables). It is run on each illusion, and a variable it removes on any illusion is removed on every illusion, so that every illusion keeps the same variables.

Exploratory Graph Analysis

EGA (EGAnet::EGA()) estimates a network of the variables (a GLASSO network of their Spearman correlations) and takes its communities (walktrap) as the dimensions. Their number comes from the data, and every variable belongs to one.

  • Stability. EGAnet::bootEGA() re-estimates it on boot_iter resamples of each illusion’s participants. It gives how often each number of dimensions comes up, and how often each variable falls in its own dimension (item stability; above 0.75 is usually taken as stable).
  • The common structure is the EGA of the illusions pooled, each variable standardised within its illusion. Each illusion’s dimensions are matched to it by their overlap, and each dimension is named after its variable with the highest network loading in the pooled analysis.

match_communities <- function(wc, ref) {
  tab <- table(wc, ref[names(wc)])
  lab <- setNames(rep(NA_character_, nrow(tab)), rownames(tab))
  while (length(tab) > 0 && max(tab) > 0) {
    ij <- which(tab == max(tab), arr.ind = TRUE)[1, ]
    lab[rownames(tab)[ij[1]]] <- colnames(tab)[ij[2]]
    tab[ij[1], ] <- 0
    tab[, ij[2]] <- 0
  }
  out <- unname(lab[as.character(wc)])
  out[is.na(out) & !is.na(wc)] <- "other"
  setNames(out, names(wc))
}

Confirmatory Factor Analysis

The common structure as a CFA (lavaan::cfa()): each variable loads on its dimension only, the dimensions are correlated, and the variables are standardised within the illusion. It is estimated by robust maximum likelihood (MLR), with full-information maximum likelihood for the missing values (a JND that could not be computed).

  • On each illusion: whether the common structure fits it. CFI and TLI above .95, RMSEA below .06 and SRMR below .08 are the usual marks of a good fit. “Admissible” is false when an estimate is out of bounds, such as a negative residual variance.
  • Across illusions: one model of every illusion’s variables, over the participants in every model, with the residuals of each variable correlated across illusions (the same participant). The configural model (the same structure on every illusion) is compared with the metric model (the same loadings too), by the scaled χ² difference and the change in CFI (a drop of more than .01 is usually read as non-invariance). Scalar invariance is not tested, since every variable is centred within study on each illusion and so has a mean of 0 by construction.

codes <- setNames(paste0("x", seq_along(dim_vars)), dim_vars)

# The model of the common structure for the illusions given by their suffixes
# ("" for one illusion alone). `equal`: the loadings equal across illusions
# (each dimension keeps its first variable as the marker). A dimension with one
# variable is that variable.
cfa_syntax <- function(suffixes, equal = FALSE) {
  lines <- c()
  for (s in suffixes) {
    for (k in names(dims)) {
      v <- codes[dims[[k]]]
      lab <- if (equal) paste0("l", k, "_", v, "*") else ""
      lines <- c(lines, paste0("D", k, s, " =~ ", paste0(lab, v, s, collapse = " + ")))
      if (length(v) == 1) lines <- c(lines, paste0(v, s, " ~~ 0*", v, s))
    }
  }
  if (length(suffixes) > 1) {
    v <- codes[unlist(dims)]
    for (p in combn(suffixes, 2, simplify = FALSE)) {
      lines <- c(lines, paste0(v, p[1], " ~~ ", v, p[2]))
    }
  }
  paste(lines, collapse = "\n")
}
fit_cfa <- function(model, data) {
  suppressWarnings(lavaan::cfa(model, data = data, estimator = "MLR", missing = "fiml"))
}
fit_indices <- function(f) {
  i <- c("cfi.robust", "tli.robust", "rmsea.robust", "srmr")
  ok <- lavaan::lavInspect(f, "converged")
  x <- if (ok) lavaan::fitMeasures(f, i) else setNames(rep(NA_real_, length(i)), i)
  tibble(Participants = lavaan::lavInspect(f, "nobs"), CFI = x[[1]], TLI = x[[2]], RMSEA = x[[3]],
         SRMR = x[[4]], Converged = ok,
         Admissible = ok && isTRUE(suppressWarnings(lavaan::lavInspect(f, "post.check"))))
}

Dimension Scores Across Illusions

Each participant’s score on each dimension, from each illusion’s own CFA (the regression method), correlated across illusions over the participants in both models (Spearman). Each illusion’s scores come from its own model, not from the joint one above. Regression scores in the joint model would be predicted partly from the other illusions’ variables, which would inflate their correlations across illusions.
